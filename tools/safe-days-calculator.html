Frequently asked questions

How effective is the safe period method?
Calendar-based methods fail for roughly 12-24 women in every 100 over a year of typical use. The Standard Days Method sits around 12 per 100 typical use, 5 per 100 with perfect use. Condoms are about 13 per 100 and the pill about 7 per 100 with typical use, so calendar methods are broadly comparable to condoms and noticeably weaker than an IUD or implant.
Are there days when pregnancy is impossible?
No. There is no day with zero chance. Sperm survive up to five days, and ovulation moves with stress, illness, travel and sleep disruption - so a day that looked low-risk when you planned it may not have been by the time it arrived. "Lower risk" is the honest description.
Is there a more accurate version of fertility awareness?
Yes. Adding basal body temperature and cervical mucus observation - the symptothermal method - drops typical-use failure from around 12-24 per 100 women per year to about 2 per 100. That is a large difference, and it comes from measuring what your body is actually doing rather than predicting from a calendar. If you are relying on fertility awareness to avoid pregnancy, learning it properly from a trained instructor is worth it.
When does the calendar method not work at all?
While breastfeeding, in the months after stopping hormonal contraception, approaching menopause, or if your cycles vary by more than about 8 days. Ovulation timing is unpredictable in all of these, so the calculation has nothing reliable to work from. Use another method.
Can I get pregnant during my period?
Yes. This is the single most common and most costly misunderstanding about the calendar method. The fertile window is set by when you ovulate, not by when you bleed - so on a short cycle, or with a long period, ovulation arrives soon enough afterwards that the fertile window overlaps the bleeding days. Sperm survive up to five days, so intercourse on a bleeding day can still meet an egg released afterwards. If your cycle is short enough for this to apply to you, the wheel above flags the exact days and the calculator says so in plain words. Being on your period is not a safe day.
How is the calendar rhythm method calculated?
The textbook formula (Ogino-Knaus) is: shortest cycle − 18 for the first fertile day, longest cycle − 11 for the last. This calculator uses − 19 rather than − 18, deliberately widening the window by one day at the start. The original formula predates the finding that sperm can survive up to five days, and at − 18 it would begin one day later than the fertile window our own ovulation calculator shows. When the goal is avoiding pregnancy, the only acceptable error is erring wide.
Can I use it with irregular periods?
No. If your shortest and longest cycles differ by more than about 8 days, calendar methods are not considered reliable and are not recommended. This calculator will tell you when your numbers fall outside that range.
What is the Standard Days Method?
A simplified version for women whose cycles are consistently 26-32 days. Days 8 through 19 are treated as fertile. It is easier to follow than full calendar rhythm, but only valid inside that cycle-length range.
Is withdrawal or douching a backup?
Withdrawal fails for about 20 in 100 women per year with typical use - it is weaker than the calendar method, not a safety net for it. Douching does not prevent pregnancy at all and can raise infection risk. If you want a genuine backup for fertile days, use condoms.
